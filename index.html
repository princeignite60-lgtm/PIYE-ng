A LANGUAGE THROUGH TIME

A short history of English

Explore some major periods in the development of the English language.

c. 450–1100

Old English

Early English developed in Britain from languages brought by Germanic-speaking peoples. Its vocabulary and grammar looked quite different from English today.

c. 1100–1500

Middle English

Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, French influenced English vocabulary, especially in law, government, food and culture.

c. 1500 onward

Modern English

Pronunciation, spelling, grammar and vocabulary changed over time. Printing helped circulate texts and encouraged more standardised written forms.

Today

English around the world

English is used in many communities and has developed many regional varieties. Migration, education, trade, science, media and the internet have shaped its global role.

c. 700–1000

Viking contact

Contact between English speakers and Scandinavian settlers brought words into English and influenced some everyday vocabulary. Words such as sky, take and they are commonly linked to Old Norse influence. Language contact often changes both vocabulary and patterns of speech.

1476 onward

Printing changes reading

William Caxton established a printing press in England in 1476. Printed books made texts easier to reproduce and circulate. Printers had to make choices about spelling and wording, helping some written forms become more familiar, although English spelling remained irregular.

1500s–1600s

Renaissance words and Shakespeare

During the Renaissance, writers and scholars borrowed and adapted words from Latin, Greek and other languages. The works of William Shakespeare helped popularise many expressions and demonstrated how English could be used for comedy, tragedy, poetry and complex ideas.

1700s–1800s

Dictionaries and standardisation

Dictionaries and grammar books became increasingly influential in describing and sometimes prescribing English usage. Samuel Johnson published a major English dictionary in 1755. Education, publishing and expanding communication helped standard written conventions spread, even as spoken varieties continued to differ.

1800s–1900s

English in a connected world

Industrialisation, migration, the British Empire, American cultural influence, international trade and later broadcasting helped English travel widely. These histories are complex and include unequal power relationships; communities also adapted English to their own identities and needs.
